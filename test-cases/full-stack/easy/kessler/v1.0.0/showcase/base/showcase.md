Kessler is an orbital demolition arcade game. A planet hangs at the middle of
the field, ringed by three orbiting bands of derelict satellites marked for
scrapping, and you ride the deflector — a curved paddle on a low circular track
just above the planet — round and round underneath them. Serve the demolition
ball outward and it climbs through the rings, chipping and breaking derelicts on
the way, until it falls back toward the planet and you have to be standing where
it lands. Miss it and it burns up below you.

What makes it its own game is that you never simply block the ball, you aim it.
Where along the deflector's span the ball lands decides the angle it leaves at:
meet it off center and it comes away turned, and every other surface it touches
— a derelict's face, the edge of a ring sweeping past, the containment field at
the top of the sky — bends it gently back toward straight out and straight back.
Lazy caroms decay into nothing. A good player puts the deflector where the
return will go into the gap they want, into the ring orbiting toward them, or
into the one derelict still standing.

The other half is the salvage. A derelict that breaks may shed a pod, and a pod
falls straight inward at a fixed speed, so it is an appointment: an angle, and a
moment. Leave the ball and go and keep it, or let it burn. A caught pod grants a
tool — a wider deflector or a narrower one, a ball that pierces straight through
whatever it meets, a shield ring around the planet that buys back one miss, or
two more balls put into the sky. The trap is that the pods you want fall while
the ball is coming home, and being in two places at once is the whole
difficulty.

A run has no clock. Sweep every ring and the wave clears for a bonus, the rings
refill, and the next wave orbits faster and pays more. Lose the last ball with
no lives left and the run ends on a score. Three lives, endless waves, and
nothing between them but how well you steer.

Because this is a full-stack case, a build owes more than the game. The planet,
the five pod sprites and the ball's six-frame spin sheet are drawn by the build
itself during the run, the destruction burst, the impact spark and the burn-up
are particle systems it makes and plays, and its thirteen sound cues and two
looping music beds are produced the same way — then wired into a game that still
has to get its polar geometry, its bounce and its pod draw exactly right.

The media here is one real run on the reference implementation: twenty-four
seconds of the first wave, played from the title screen on the keyboard, with a
single ball served and never lost. Twenty-six returns off the deflector take
forty of the wave's forty-eight derelicts apart, sixteen salvage pods fall out
of the wreckage and ten are caught — enough to raise the shield, widen the
deflector, set the balls piercing, and stack six of them in the sky at once —
and the clip settles at 9,350 points with all three lives intact and eight
derelicts still standing. The three stills are frames of that same run.
