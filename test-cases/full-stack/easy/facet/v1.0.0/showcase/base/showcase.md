Facet is a match-three puzzle on a lapidary's lit bench. Eight columns by eight
rows of cut stones fill the board. Take hold of a stone, carry it onto the one
beside it and let go: a line of three or more of a kind shatters, the stones
above fall into the gap, and fresh ones drop in from the top, so one move can
touch off a chain that runs long after your hand has left the board. Nothing is
committed until you let go, so carrying a stone back where it came from takes the
move back.

What makes it its own game is strain. Every clear stresses the stones left
standing around it, and that stress never lets go: a stone carries its cracks
for the rest of the round and takes them with it when it falls. A stone cracked
through is flawed, and a flawed stone goes whenever anything beside it goes.
Ordinary play primes the board a little at a time, and a swap dropped into a
well-primed corner tears a hole clean through it. Three cuts sit on top of that:
a line of four leaves a brilliant that takes its neighbors, a line crossing
another leaves a star that takes its whole row and column, and a line of five or
more leaves a prism that takes every stone of whatever kind it is traded against.

A round has no clock. A meter fills as stones clear, each level asking more than
the last and totalling up its longest chain and its best single move before the
next board pours in from above, and the round ends when no move is left that
would shatter anything.

A build carries the whole bench — the nine rules and the chain that resolves
them, the seeded deal, a move offered and withdrawn under the hand until a
release commits it, and six screens worked equally by a mouse, a pen or a finger
— and, because this is a full-stack case, it also draws every stone, effect and
sound it plays as a file it produced during the run.

The media here is one real round on the reference implementation: twenty-four
seconds played with a real mouse, fourteen moves and twenty-seven chain steps,
one of which took twenty-two stones off the board at once, ending on a settled
board at level two. The stills are frames of that same round.
