Orrery is a machine-building puzzle played on a field of ninety-one hexes under
a dark sky. A challenge names what it wants — constellations of celestial motes,
made out of the reagents it hands you — and everything between the two is a
machine you engrave and assemble yourself: sigils cut into the field, brass arms
standing over them, and a looping tape of instructions written for each arm.

The tape is the whole of the game's language. Ten instructions, no branches and
no conditions: grab and drop, rotate and pivot, extend and retract, advance and
recede. Every arm runs its own tape on one period shared by the whole machine,
cycle for cycle, forever, so an arm that grabs on cycle three and lets go on
cycle five will do it again on ten and twelve. One arm is easy. Getting five of
them to hand motes to each other on the same beat, and never to swing two motes
into the same place at the same moment, is the game.

Sigils are what change what you are carrying. A `wane` cools an essence resting
on it down to dust. A `bind` writes a filament between two motes and makes them
one rigid constellation; a `sunder` cuts one back apart. A `confluence` sits
with four founts around a crown, and the moment all four hold one of each
essence it folds them into a single aether. They act at the boundary between
cycles, on whatever happens to be standing on them, so a machine is a route as
much as a mechanism — the arm carries the mote over the engraving and the
engraving does the rest.

Then you press play and it runs without you. The machine has to deliver its
product six times over without faulting, and a fault is usually two motes
arriving in the same place: the run freezes on the instant it happened and marks
the parts that did it. Finish, and the machine is scored three ways at once —
what it cost, how many cycles it took, and how much of the field it used — with
every one of them better lower. There is always a smaller machine.
